## Where are we on realising the right to free education for all children in India?

Behind this deceptively straightforward question are three complicated terms: *‘a right’, ‘free’, and ‘education’*. As we unpack these terms, it will become clear why this goal is challenging to meet.

A right is a claim/entitlement that creates a corollary duty on others. Some rights, like the right to one’s property, require others to *refrain from* taking what is yours. Such rights (called negative rights) allow you to go to work every day, safe in the knowledge that when you come home, your house will still be yours. Other rights, like the right to education, are *positive rights* and require someone to proactively provide you with something. Typically, the government fulfils such duties and provides people access to benefits using taxpayer money. While no one is coerced to tutor another person’s child, we all incur the cost when the government takes this responsibility. Even the beneficiaries of such free services pay for them via indirect taxes on essentials.

And ‘education’? One can seek education anywhere: at home, in libraries, online, on the playground, and even at parties if one is lucky. Typically, though, when people refer to the right to education, they mean the right to access *schooling*.

So, where is India on the right to free education for all children? We have made education a *fundamental right*. The government has set up buildings and the supporting paraphernalia to provide millions access to schools for free. We have achieved *near-universal enrollment in primary schools*. Sadly, we are behind on *education*.

In 2009, India ranked second-to-last out of 74 countries in a test that evaluates students’ reading, mathematical, and scientific literacy (Programme for International Student Assessment). Unfortunately, instead of overcoming this learning crisis, we opted out of subsequent assessments and continue to do so.

The free government schools established to realise the right to education are notorious for their abysmal quality, despite heavy investments. Depending on the state, the annual per-child expenditure ranges from around ₹15,000 to over ₹80,000. In Delhi, for instance, it was ~₹69,736 in 2021–22. The result? Schools with poor infrastructure and unsafe buildings, classrooms without teachers (per some estimates, 23.6% teachers were absent during unannounced school visits), and students who are not learning (only ~28% of Grade 3 students can solve a subtraction problem and ~23% can read a Grade 2-level text). In fact, even though the budget for school education has increased by 78% between 2014 and 2023, gains in learning during this period have been minimal.
