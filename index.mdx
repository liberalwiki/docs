## A Chai Stall and a Question

It started, as many good things in India do, over chai.

A few of us were sitting outside a small stall near Connaught Place — the kind with plastic stools and tea that costs twelve rupees — arguing about why a street vendor a few feet away had been fined for operating without a license. The man had been selling *golgappas* on that corner for eleven years. He knew his customers by name. He was feeding his family. And yet, every few weeks, a municipal officer would show up, pocket some cash, and leave.

One of us asked: *"Why does this keep happening?"*

The answer, we realised, was not that the officer was particularly corrupt or that the vendor was particularly unlucky. The answer was a system — one built on the assumption that people cannot be trusted to make their own choices, that markets are dangerous without enough supervision, and that the state is always better placed than individuals to decide what is good for them.

We disagreed. Strongly.

---

## What We Believe

We are free market libertarians. That means we believe in the radical — and we use that word deliberately — idea that **people are capable of running their own lives**.

We believe that when individuals are free to trade, to create, to fail, and to try again, they produce more prosperity, more innovation, and more dignity than any central planner ever could. We believe that prices carry information no bureaucrat possesses. We believe that voluntary exchange, even between strangers, builds trust and cooperation in ways that regulation rarely achieves.

We are not against all rules. We are against rules that exist primarily to protect the powerful from competition, to extract rent from the productive, or to substitute the state's judgment for yours.

We believe the golgappa vendor deserved better. And so do millions of others like him.

## Why a Wiki?

Ideas need infrastructure.

Libertarian thinking has a rich tradition — from Adam Smith's insight that the baker's self-interest feeds us, to Hayek's warning about the fatal conceit of central planning, to the long and ongoing work of economists, philosophers, and activists across the world who have made the case for human freedom.

But in India, these ideas often feel distant. They arrive wrapped in Western contexts, citing American policy debates or European economic history. They can feel theoretical when the problems in front of us are immediate: Why are food prices controlled in ways that hurt farmers? Why does starting a business require dozens of permissions? Why do our best public schools consistently underperform private ones that charge a fraction of the cost?

We built this wiki to **bring these ideas home**.

Not to preach. Not to hand down a doctrine. But to build a living, honest, evolving resource that takes real Indian questions seriously and tries to answer them from first principles — with evidence, with humility, and with a genuine belief that freedom works.

## Who We Are

We are students, researchers, writers, and people who got into arguments at chai stalls and never quite stopped.

We are affiliated with the [Centre for Civil Society](https://ccs.in), one of India's oldest free market think tanks, which has spent decades working on education reform, economic freedom, and policy research.

We do not agree on everything. Some of us are more skeptical of the state than others. Some of us think markets need more complementary institutions; others think the biggest problem is simply that markets aren't free enough. We argue about this constantly.

But we agree on the foundations: **that individuals matter, that voluntary cooperation is powerful, and that the ideas of liberty deserve a serious, accessible home in the Indian public conversation.**

This wiki is that home. We hope you'll help us build it.
