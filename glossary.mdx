A reference for terms that come up often in policy debates. These are not neutral definitions — they are written from a free market perspective, which means where there is genuine disagreement, we say so.

---

## Price Signals

Prices are not just numbers. In a free market, a price carries compressed information about scarcity, demand, production costs, and the preferences of millions of people who have never met each other. When the price of onions rises, it tells farmers to grow more, consumers to use less, and traders to move supply from surplus regions to deficit ones — all without a single committee meeting.

When governments fix prices (as with India's essential commodities controls), they don't eliminate this information — they suppress it. The result is shortages, black markets, and resources flowing to the wrong places.

**See also:** Spontaneous Order, Seen and Unseen

## Spontaneous Order

One of the most counterintuitive ideas in economics: complex, functional systems can emerge from the independent decisions of many individuals, without anyone designing or directing them.

The English language has no author. Cities develop their own logic without a master plan. Markets coordinate the production and distribution of millions of goods without a central authority. Friedrich Hayek called this *spontaneous order* — the product of human action, but not of human design.

This is why libertarians are skeptical of top-down planning: not because planners are stupid, but because the knowledge required to plan an economy is dispersed across millions of minds and can never be fully collected in one place.

## Rent-Seeking

When a business or individual uses political connections or lobbying to gain an advantage — rather than creating actual value — they are *rent-seeking*. The term comes from economics but the behaviour is instantly recognisable: getting a law passed that blocks a competitor, securing a government contract through relationships rather than merit, or obtaining a licence that protects an existing business from new entrants.

Rent-seeking is expensive for society. Resources that could go into innovation or production go instead into influencing policy. The Indian *licence raj* was, in large part, a rent-seeking machine — industries lobbied for protections that enriched incumbents at the expense of consumers and new competitors.

**See also:** Regulatory Capture

## Regulatory Capture

A regulator is created to protect the public interest. Over time, the industry it regulates gains disproportionate influence over it. Eventually, the regulator acts primarily in the interest of the regulated industry — not the public it was meant to serve. This is *regulatory capture*.

It is not usually the result of explicit corruption. It happens because industry insiders have more knowledge, more resources, and more sustained interest in regulatory decisions than any individual citizen does. The result: barriers to entry get raised, competition is suppressed, and consumers pay more.

This is one reason libertarians are cautious about creating new regulators as a solution to every market problem: regulators frequently become the problem.

## Comparative Advantage

You might be better than your neighbour at both cooking and plumbing. But if you are *much* better at cooking and only *slightly* better at plumbing, you should cook and hire a plumber — even if you could do the job better yourself. This is the principle of *comparative advantage*, and it applies to nations as much as individuals.

India does not need to manufacture everything it consumes to be prosperous. It needs to specialise in what it produces at relatively lower cost, trade for the rest, and let the gains from specialisation raise living standards. Protectionist policies that try to make India self-sufficient in every sector violate this logic and make everyone poorer.

## Seen and Unseen

The French economist Frédéric Bastiat identified one of the most persistent errors in economic thinking: we see the effects of a policy that are immediate and visible; we do not see the effects that are hidden, delayed, or counterfactual.

A tariff on imported steel *visibly* saves jobs in domestic steel mills. What is *unseen* is the higher cost paid by every manufacturer who uses steel — the cars that cost more, the construction that gets delayed, the jobs that never exist in downstream industries.

Good economic thinking requires asking not just *"what do we see?"* but *"what would have happened otherwise?"*

## Creative Destruction

Coined by Joseph Schumpeter: the process by which new technologies, products, and businesses continuously displace old ones. The car destroyed the horse-carriage industry. The smartphone devastated camera manufacturers. E-commerce upended physical retail.

This destruction is not a flaw in capitalism — it is the engine of progress. The living standards we enjoy today exist because older, less efficient ways of doing things were replaced. Policies that protect incumbent industries from competition — in the name of saving jobs — slow this process and, in the long run, make everyone worse off.

The correct response to creative destruction is not to stop it but to ensure people have the freedom and flexibility to adapt.

## Moral Hazard

When someone is insulated from the consequences of their decisions, they take more risk than they otherwise would. This is *moral hazard*.

A bank that knows it will be bailed out by the government takes riskier bets. A farmer who knows crop insurance will cover all losses plants less carefully. A driver with comprehensive insurance is less cautious than one who bears the full cost of an accident.

Moral hazard is not about morality in the everyday sense — it is about incentives. It is one of the strongest arguments for letting markets allocate risk: people who bear the cost of their choices tend to make better ones.

## Public Choice Theory

Economics applied to political decision-making. The central insight: politicians, bureaucrats, and regulators are not selfless servants of the public interest — they are people with their own incentives, careers, and interests, responding to the pressures of the political system.

Voters are rationally ignorant (the cost of becoming informed about policy exceeds the benefit of one vote). Politicians respond to concentrated, organised interests rather than diffuse public ones. Bureaucracies expand because larger agencies mean more power and budget, regardless of outcomes.

Public choice theory explains why good policy is so hard to achieve even when everyone knows what good policy looks like. It is a powerful argument for limiting what governments are asked to do in the first place.

**See also:** Regulatory Capture, Rent-Seeking

## Opportunity Cost

Every choice forecloses another. When the government spends ₹10,000 crore on a subsidy programme, the *opportunity cost* is everything else that money could have done — tax relief, infrastructure, or simply staying in the pockets of the people who earned it. When a farmer's land is zoned for agriculture only, the opportunity cost is the factory, housing, or business that could have been built there.

Opportunity cost is invisible, which is why it is so often ignored. Politicians celebrate what they build; they never point to what was never built because the resources went elsewhere. Sound economic thinking requires holding both in view at once.
